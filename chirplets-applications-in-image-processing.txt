Rather than treating image deformations through basic rotations or translations, it models complex geometric changes—like perspective tilts and radar chirps—as generalized coordinate transformations.

🗺️ The Core Mathematical Framework

Traditional signal processing maps 1D audio or 2D image coordinates using simple translations and scaling (the Wavelet Transform). The PCT and MCT extend this by introducing chirps—signals or image spatial frequencies that increase or decrease linearly or projectively over distance.

1. Projective Chirplet Transform (PCT) Subspaces

The PCT bridges signal processing with projective geometry (camera perspectives).
• The Concept: When a camera views a flat grid at an angle, the grid lines appear to compress as they move toward the horizon. This spatial frequency compression acts exactly like a chirp.
• Subspaces: The transform computes a specific subspace of a higher-dimensional parameter space (such as the projective group \(SL(3, \mathbb{R})\)). It limits the computation to specific coordinates (e.g., horizontal/vertical tilts or pan-tilt-zoom paths).
• Application: Image rectification, horizon tracking, and perspective correction. It allows an algorithm to cleanly un-warp a skewed document or billboard into a flat, front-facing image.

2. Metaplectic Chirplet Transform (MCT) Subspaces

The MCT bridges signal processing with quantum mechanics and optics via the Metaplectic Group (a double cover of the Symplectic Group).
• The Concept: It models coordinate distortions that involve linear chirping—such as spatial shears, rotations, and quadratic phase modulations (similar to how light bends through a lens).
• Subspaces: Computing the entire MCT hyperspace is computationally demanding. By isolating specific subspaces, researchers extract only the localized orientation and acceleration of the image components.
• Application: Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) processing and motion blur removal. It models the acceleration or Doppler shifts of objects moving across an image sensor.
